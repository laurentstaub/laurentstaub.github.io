Over the years I have read, built and fixed a fair number of business plans. Ambitious ones, cautious ones, ones bristling with tabs and macros. They all had one thing in common: people talked to me a great deal about their numbers, and very little about what held those numbers up. The modest ambition of this article is to offer you a change of perspective: stop reading a business plan as a numerical prediction, and start reading it as what it really is — **a list of assumptions dressed up as a spreadsheet**.


## The numbers are only consequences


Say you want to launch a food truck. Your business plan proudly announces: 22 working days per month, 60 baskets served per day, €12 per basket. That makes €15,840 in monthly revenue. Your spreadsheet will hand you that result with two decimal places if you ask. It may be wrong, but it will have two decimal places.


Where do those three numbers come from? The 22 assumes you will get the pitch and the truck will run every working day. The 60 assumes customers will come — and come back. The 12 assumes they will accept that price without blinking. Three bets. The 15,840, on the other hand, is not information: it is **the arithmetic consequence of three bets**.

And this is true of every cell in the file. Year-3 EBITDA is probably one of the most scrutinized and least informative numbers there is: a stack of dozens of bets, multiplied by one another. As long as those bets stay implicit, the plan looks solid. Which is precisely what should worry you.


## The cell test


The right reflex fits in one question. Take any number in the plan and ask: **what has to be true for this cell to tell the truth?** Ask it three or four times in a row and you will see things surface that the spreadsheet does not show:



- 20&nbsp;% annual growth assumes your competitors will watch you do it without reacting;
- a stable margin assumes your suppliers will not raise their prices — ask them what they think;
- a spring launch assumes everything that needs to be ready beforehand will be, which is already a dozen cascading assumptions.


You will notice, in passing, that the most dangerous assumptions are usually **not in the spreadsheet at all**: the permit that never arrives, the key person who leaves, the single supplier that shuts down. The spreadsheet only computes what it is given.


## An assumption worthy of the name is falsifiable


Once the assumptions are extracted, you still have to write them properly. Most assumptions found in business plans are unassailable — and that is exactly the problem. "The market is buoyant" cannot be contradicted by reality; the sentence risks nothing, and therefore teaches you nothing. Compare:



- "The market is buoyant" → "at least 200 people walk past the pitch between 12pm and 2pm, and one in ten stops";
- "Customers will show up" → "we serve 60 baskets a day from month three";
- "We will be operational quickly" → "the truck is fitted out and certified within eight weeks".


The left-hand version is a slogan. The right-hand version can be contradicted by reality — which is exactly what we ask of it. A well-formulated assumption contains **a number, a deadline, and a failure condition**: if any of the three is missing, you are holding an opinion, not an assumption.



> A business plan does not die from its calculations. It dies from its assumptions.


## Not all assumptions are equal


Your list will quickly grow to fifteen or twenty lines, and you will have neither the time nor the money to verify everything. Two questions are enough to sort it: **if this assumption breaks, does the plan survive?** And: **how much does checking it cost?** A critical, uncertain assumption gets tested right away. A critical but well-documented assumption gets monitored. A secondary assumption goes on the list and waits its turn.


And to test it, there is no need to commission a five-figure market study. As with [data analysis](@root/en/articles/analyzing-data-in-excel.html), the minimum data necessary is enough. Want to check the food truck's demand bet? Go count the passers-by on a Tuesday between noon and two. It is free, it is slightly boring, and it is worth all the slides in the world.


## Track your assumptions the way you track a budget


Surfacing and testing is good. But a business plan lives on, and its assumptions age — some well, some badly. The discipline that changes everything is modest: at every monthly or quarterly review, reread the list. For each assumption: still true, weakened, or contradicted. Green, amber, red. Ten minutes.


The day a critical assumption turns red, the decision — continue, pivot, stop — is no longer a matter of ego or internal politics: **it has been written into the list from the start**. That discipline — surface, then test, then track — is what separates a plan you endure from a plan you steer. It is, as it happens, the heart of [our approach](@root/en/approach.html).


Next time someone hands you a business plan, resist the urge to ask "how much?". Ask instead: "what has to be true?". You will see — the conversation immediately changes in nature.
