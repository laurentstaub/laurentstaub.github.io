Business plans you can open

I hold a doctorate in pharmacy, and I build the business plans, manufacturing cost models and pricing analyses of small and mid-sized pharmaceutical companies. Every number traces back to a named assumption — so it can be discussed, tested and revised.

What I do

Four ways into a plan

Blocks of a few weeks, working directly with the finance or development function. The output is a model and a set of slides.

Business plan and profit and loss

Consolidate sales, rebuild margins, project five years out. A model where every line traces back to the assumption that produces it, and scenarios that show which one breaks first.

Manufacturing capacity and costs

Processes, batch sizes, equipment, allocation between sites: turning a sales plan into machine hours and cost of goods. Break-even by product, and bottlenecks identified before the investment, not after.

Pricing and market access

Price sensitivity, treated population, reimbursement and public procurement constraints. What price does to volume, what volume does to price, and where the defensible balance sits.

Due diligence and investment decisions

Read a dataroom, rebuild the vendor's model rather than re-read it, isolate the break-even point, write down the questions that remain open. The output is a reasoned decision rather than an impression.

My conviction

A plan is only as good as its assumptions

Every project and every business plan rests on assumptions — about markets, costs, regulatory timelines, pricing and reimbursement. Most remain implicit, and that is where plans drift. I put them back at the centre.

Surface

Write down, in black and white, the assumptions the plan depends on: what has to be true for this to work?

Test

Confront each assumption with the available data — yours and the market's — to separate what is proven from what is hoped.

Track

Follow assumptions over time the way you follow a budget: decisions to continue, pivot or stop become factual.

Tooling

AI and data are a means, not a deliverable

I write the tools these models need: project databases, dashboards, consolidation scripts, and machine learning where it saves time on a repetitive task — a literature watch, a data cleaning job. Nobody buys a model: what clients buy is a business plan that is right and a cost of goods that stands up. The tooling belongs to the production chain, not to the invoice.

Where I work

The pharmaceutical industry, and its international side

Long, regulated cycles, administered prices, negotiated market access, supply tensions, sensitive data. I know the sector from the inside, from manufacturing to the community pharmacy — where I still practise.

The plans I build rarely stop at one country: portfolios of licensing and supply agreements across some forty countries, manufacturing sites in Europe and India, pricing policy and public procurement in China. Geography is part of the model.
