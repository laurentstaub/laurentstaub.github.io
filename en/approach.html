My approach

Steering by assumptions

Behind every business plan, every cost of goods, every acquisition file, there are bets on the future. I make them visible, testable and tracked over time.

Why assumptions?

A business plan is not a prediction: it is a chain of assumptions — about the market, prices, costs, timelines, adoption. The numbers it produces are merely the arithmetic consequences of those bets. As long as the assumptions stay implicit, the plan looks solid; that is precisely where plans drift.

When a project fails, it is rarely the calculations that were wrong. It is assumptions that were never written down, never confronted with data, never re-examined when the context changed. Conversely, a team that knows at any moment what has to be true for its plan to work — and where each of those conditions stands — can make decisions quickly and without drama.

At the start of any exploration there is a question to answer — sometimes reduced to a single assumption.

The three phases of the method

1. Surface

I start by extracting the assumptions from the plan: the ones that appear in the model, and above all the ones that do not. Each is stated in falsifiable terms — "the market will accept this price", "the line will hold forty batches a year", "this channel will generate 30 % of sales" — then ranked by its impact on the outcome and its degree of uncertainty.

2. Test

Each critical assumption is then confronted with the available data: yours — sales, processes, standard costs, contracts — and the market's. Some can be checked in a spreadsheet in a day; others call for a study, a measurement on site or a prototype. What matters is separating what is proven from what is hoped, and spending validation effort where the stakes justify it.

3. Track

Finally, assumptions are tracked over time, the way a budget is. At every review, the question is not only "how is the schedule?" but "do our assumptions still hold?". Decisions to continue, pivot or stop become factual, not political.

In practice

The method applies to four types of engagement:

  • Business plan and profit and loss — consolidating sales, rebuilding margins, projecting five years out, and scenarios that show which assumption breaks first.
  • Manufacturing capacity and costs — processes, batch sizes, equipment, allocation between sites: the sales plan translated into machine hours, cost of goods and break-even by product.
  • Pricing and market access — price sensitivity, treated population, reimbursement and public procurement constraints, and the effect of each on the outcome of the plan.
  • Due diligence and investment decisions — reading the dataroom, rebuilding the vendor's model rather than re-reading it, isolating the break-even point, and listing the questions that remain open.

What the tooling changes, and what it does not

I write the tools these models need: project databases, dashboards, consolidation scripts, and machine learning where it saves time on a repetitive task. It is a means of production, not a deliverable: what gets discussed in the meeting is still a business plan that is right and a cost of goods that stands up.

What makes this practice singular is the bridge between analytical rigour and the investment decision: I build the financial model and the data infrastructure that lets it be confronted with reality.
